The house is sold after repair at £550k, giving £207,549 net equity. Heather gets **62.5% (£129,718)**. Each keeps their own pensions. Clean break.

**Rupert:** equity £77,831 − personal debts £7,099 = **£70,732 cash**, plus pensions **£220,788**. Total ~£291.5k. (Heather: £129,718 + £195,508 = £325.2k.)

**The proposal this implies:** "Heather takes 62.5% of the net equity (~£130k at a £550k sale). Each keeps their own pensions. Clean break, no maintenance either way." That's option B (£116k) plus ~£13k in exchange for the clean break.

## Housing (£250k home, 15 years at 4.5%)

- Without pension tax-free cash: mortgage £184,768, **£1,413/mo**.
- Using ~£55k tax-free cash: mortgage £129,571, **£991/mo**, and the pension pot falls to ~£165.6k.
- **Can he get it?** On £116k gross, £185k is ~1.6× income, so the amount itself is easy. The catch is age. A 15-year term runs to 75, and the lender will test whether retirement income covers payments after ~67. Projected retirement income (state pension ~£12.5k + drawdown ~£8–9k) may not pass that test. A lender could then cap the term at retirement: £185k over 7 years ≈ **£2,570/mo**. Broker check needed.

## Findings

- A ~£1,400/mo mortgage is fine while he's on his current salary (23% of net).
- **Redundancy at 63 with no further work breaks it.** The pension pot runs out at about 74–75.
- It becomes survivable with **any one** of: a modest job (~£25k gross) until 67; Mandy sharing the mortgage; or a smaller mortgage (cheaper home, or bigger deposit).
- Every extra £10k to Heather above option B adds ~£76/mo to his mortgage for 15 years.
- Implication: a 60–65% tilt only makes sense if it buys a clean break. It's worth pricing a plan B (smaller home, or renting) before agreeing to anything above ~62.5%.
