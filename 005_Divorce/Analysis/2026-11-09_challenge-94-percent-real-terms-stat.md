# Challenge: is "80% of the equity = 94% in real terms" a fair statistic? — 9 Nov 2026

*Not legal advice. Private: strategy, not for Heather.* Follows [[2026-11-06_equity-split-real-terms]].

## Question

Rupert asked for a hard challenge on the 6 Nov working. It said that if lifetime contributions were 80/20, Heather's ask for 80% of the equity gives her ~94% "in real terms". Is that a reasonable statistic, or is it doing strange things with numbers?

## Verdict

The arithmetic is right, but the statistic shouldn't be used. Three reasons:

1. **The metric inflates everything.** "Real-terms share" is a ratio of returns: Heather 4.00 per £1 against Rupert 0.25 per £1, a 16:1 ratio, which comes out as 94%. Any split looks extreme on this measure. A plain **50/50 split already scores Heather "80%"**. A measure that calls equal division 80/20 against her proves too much. It doesn't describe a share of anything real. Nobody gets 94% of any pot. Said to Heather or a solicitor, "94% of everything" is untrue as stated and easy to knock down.
2. **The 80/20 input isn't a lifetime figure.** 80/20 is the interim post-separation split of the Pebmarsh running costs. Lifetime contributions (deposit, 27 years of mortgage and savings) haven't been worked out. Heather also ran the home and raised three children, and the law counts that as an equal contribution.
3. **Contributions are the wrong legal frame for a long marriage.** In England and Wales a 27-year marriage (40 years together) starts from **equal sharing, regardless of who earned what** (*White v White*, no discrimination between breadwinner and homemaker). Courts depart from 50/50 on **needs**, almost never on financial contribution. The "special contribution" bar is very high and earning more in a salaried job doesn't meet it. An argument built on "I put in 80%" invites the reply that the law doesn't count that way, and it reads as hostile.

## The honest versions of the point

- **Plain:** 80% to Heather means she receives 4× her share of the inputs and Rupert keeps a quarter of his (on 80/20, which is unproven).
- **Better (whole pot, not equity alone):** this is the frame the court will actually use.

Using the D81 Q10 basis: £550k sale − mortgage £309,748.40 − costs £9,000 − excess £1,000 − joint debts £22,702.95 = **£207,548.65 net equity**. Pensions: Rupert £220,787.88, Heather £195,507.83 (self-reported).

| Equity split (H / R) | Heather total | Rupert total | Heather's share of everything |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 50 / 50 | £299,282 | £324,562 | 48.0% |
| 56.1 / 43.9 | £311,922 | £311,922 | **50.0%** |
| 80 / 20 | £361,547 | £262,298 | **58.0%** |

So an 80% equity split gives Heather **~58% of the total assets**, not 94%. That's a true, defensible figure. And because Rupert's pensions are ~£25k higher, an **exactly equal overall split already gives Heather ~56% of the equity**.

Caveats on the table:
- Heather's pensions are self-reported, with no CETVs (cash equivalent transfer values) yet.
- Two of her four pensions are defined benefit (Prudential staff scheme and LGPS, the local government scheme). Their CETVs often understate what they're really worth, which would favour Rupert.
- A pound of pension isn't worth a pound of cash: it's taxed and locked until retirement. Offsetting usually discounts pension value, which favours Heather.
- Rupert's personal debts (~£7,099) aren't included.

## Devil's advocate: Heather's side

- **Needs.** On £1,587/mo net she can borrow little: ~£100k at 4.5× her £23k gross, if a lender will go that far at 59. With £104k equity she has ~£200k to buy with. At 80% (£166k) she'd have ~£266k. Rupert nets ~4× her income and can borrow far more. A court looking at housing needs could well give her **more than 50% of the equity**. She has probably been advised of exactly this.
- **Income gap plus the clean break.** A larger capital share is the usual price of a clean break when incomes are this far apart. 60–65% of the equity in exchange for no spousal maintenance is a plausible outcome.
- **Lucy.** Her 2-bedroom argument (29 Oct) is a needs point, weak as Lucy is 20 and has her own Norwich tenancy, but it's the line she's on.

## Rupert's side

- Whole-pot view: 80% of the equity is ~58% of everything, before her DB pensions are valued properly.
- Rupert has needs too. He's at Westgate renting and has to rehouse at 60.
- Rupert is carrying 80% of the Pebmarsh costs (~£4k/mo combined outgoings) during the interim.
- A need for housing is a reason to look at structure: a modest tilt in her favour, or a deferred sale or buyout ([[Heather Buyout Scenario]]). It isn't a reason for an 80/20 headline.

## Recommended actions

- Drop the 94% figure. Don't use it with Heather or Alan.
- If a number is needed, use the whole-pot share (~58%) and the "equal overall = ~56% of the equity" point.
- Get Heather's formal CETVs, as this is what the whole-pot argument rests on.
- Model her actual housing need: mortgage capacity plus equity against 2-bed prices near Pebmarsh/Halstead. Find what equity share meets it.
- Ask Alan where a court would likely land on needs. Add to [[Questions for Alan]].

## Follow-up (9 Nov): Rupert's points

- **80/20 is a lifetime earnings ratio** (Rupert's correction). He earned ~80% throughout, and Heather didn't work for ~15 years while raising the children. Accepted as fact. Rupert accepts contributions aren't the argument and has dropped it. He also says he doesn't want her penalised for the career break, which is exactly what equal sharing is for.
- **Does she have a "right to buy"?** No automatic right. Housing is the first need the court looks at for *both* parties. It tries to meet both from the resources available, judged against the standard of living during the marriage. Renting at Westgate doesn't cut Rupert's own housing need. Where resources won't stretch to both, the party with the lower earning capacity tends to be prioritised for capital, because Rupert can borrow and she can't.
- **Her inheritance.** Expected inheritances from living parents are very rarely counted: wills can change and care costs can eat the estate. Weight: low, and it can't be relied on. The same principle keeps Rupert's own future inheritances out (see memory: inheritances excluded), so raising hers invites the mirror argument.
- **How far ahead?** With a clean break the settlement has to meet her needs for life, including retirement. That is why pensions matter. Long-marriage settlements often aim at roughly comparable retirement incomes.
- **No pension sharing (Heather's current loose position).** This means offsetting: each keeps their own pensions. It fits the whole-pot table, where an equal overall split is ~56% of the equity to her. Caveat: her DB pensions (Prudential staff, LGPS) may be worth more than their CETVs, and Alan may suggest an actuarial report (from a PODE, a pensions-on-divorce expert).

## The clean-break lens

The gap between an equal overall split (56.1%) and her ask (80%) is ~24% of £207,548.65 = **~£49.6k**. As a comparison point: spousal maintenance of £500/mo until her state pension age (67, Dec 2033, ~7 years) would cost ~£42k, and a joint-lives order would cost more. So part of her ask can be seen as the price of a clean break. The useful question for Rupert is **what a clean break is worth to him**, not what's "fair" on contributions. That gives a negotiating range of roughly 56% (equal) to the mid-60s% (equal plus a clean-break premium). 80% is well above it.
